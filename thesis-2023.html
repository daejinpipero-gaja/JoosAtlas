Master's thesis · Yonsei University · June 2023

Can a camera tell welding from fire?

My thesis, Detection of Fire Hazard Factors on Construction Sites using YOLOv5, read here in plain language: what the problem was, what I built, what went wrong first, and what the numbers do and do not show.

01 Problem

The fire starts as work.

On a construction site the leading cause of fire is a spark from welding or cutting. When that spark lands in flammable vapour, urethane foam, paint or fuel, the fire grows far faster than an ordinary one.

In April 2020 a fire at a logistics-centre construction site in Icheon killed 38 people. It is believed to have started when welding sparks reached combustible material while urethane foam was being applied in the basement and a cargo lift was being welded in.

Construction-site fires per year

4,⁠008 fires in five years, about 801 a year.

Source: National Fire Data System of the National Fire Agency, as cited in Table 1.1 of the thesis. The film's 2,⁠732 is the count for a later period, 2020 to 2024.

Why the usual detector does not fit

Dust and weldingHeat and smoke detectors misfire.

Where welding and dust are part of the job, a detector that senses heat and smoke trips often or loses sensitivity.

Open spaceThey do not suit large open areas.

They work inside the range of the sensor. A half-built floor without walls is not that.

A site that changesNo fixed zone to protect.

Structure and layout change day by day, so the detection zones an automatic system needs cannot be settled.

02 Idea

Three classes instead of one alarm.

Earlier vision-based work asked a single question: is there a fire? On a site where welding is the job, that question raises an alarm all day. I trained the detector to name three things at once, so that work is reported as work and flame as flame.

FireThe flame itself

The box is drawn tightly on the flame.

WeldingThe core where the scatter begins

Not the whole shower of sparks. Section 03 explains why that distinction decided the result.

EngineerA person

Counted when the head or upper body is visible, usually with arms or legs. A pair of legs alone, or a single limb, is not. The camera is assumed not to look up from below.

Why learn from pictures instead of calculating

Arc welding produces heat of roughly 5,⁠000 °C at the arc, and throws spatter 0.3 to 3 mm across at more than 1,⁠600 °C. Korean and United States guidance both keep ignitable material 11 m (35 ft) from the work.

I filmed welding outdoors on bare concrete at −3 °C in a 1 m/s wind, at about 60 frames a second, and measured the spatter against a hand in the frame. Ejection speeds ran from 1.76 to 5 m/s. I could not establish a stable relation between direction, speed and distance.

A flame or a spark has no fixed shape and no quantity that predicts it. That is the reason for a detector that learns what they look like.

03 Data

The first labels taught it the background.

I collected about 2,⁠000 images for each class, 6,⁠000 in all, from construction sites, fire scenes, experiments and broadcast or online video, then tripled them by augmentation to 18,⁠000. Seventy per cent trained the model, twenty validated it, ten tested it.

At first I drew one box around the whole shower of sparks, core and scatter together. Most of that box was background, and the model learned the box.

69%of background was called Welding
57%of Welding was missed as background
43%of Welding was detected correctly

What I tried that did not solve it

  • Filming at night to quieten the background. The welding flash lit it anyway.
  • Outlining the sparks with polygons. No real difference from boxes.
  • One box per spatter. The features were inconsistent and the labour large.

What did

  • Box the core where the scatter begins, and the flame itself. Keep every box tight.
  • Remove images where the object hides behind structure, handrails, poles or lines.
  • Correct wrong class labels, drop blurred and cropped frames, balance the three classes.

Focal Loss, and how much it helped

A one-stage detector sees far more background than object, so easy background examples dominate the loss. Focal Loss turns their weight down so that training attends to the hard cases.

FL(pt) = −αt (1 − pt)γ log(pt)

I tested α from 0.1 to 0.5 and γ from 1.0 to 5.0. The best pair was α = 0.25, γ = 2.0. The effect was small: correct detections rose by 0.02, misses to background fell by 0.02, and the mean F1 stayed at 0.78.

Augmentation, which had tripled the set, lowered recognition on this data in later runs, and the original images became the primary set. Dropout and early stopping were used to keep the model general.

What raised the detection rate most was better original data. It was worth more than any option or adjustment applied afterwards.

04 Findings

Eight models, one finding.

After the dataset was repaired I trained eight sizes of YOLOv5 for 500 epochs each. They find the objects about equally well. What a larger model buys is a tighter box, and it costs many times the training.

The reading I drew from this: for a camera on a site, the small model is enough to notice, and light enough to run on modest hardware.

Input size was settled in a preliminary run. At 256 pixels small objects were lost; at 1,⁠920 precision fell; 640, 800 and 1,⁠024 behaved alike, and 640 was the steadiest.

Against YOLOv8, newly released at the time, on the same data and settings for 100 epochs: peak mAP50 0.795 for YOLOv5 and 0.799 for YOLOv8.

On video the model had never seen, a news broadcast and firefighting footage, it found welding, people and flame, at lower confidence than on its own held-out images.

05 Limits

What the thesis does not show.

  • It detects. It does not raise an alarm.The model marks where flame, welding and people are. A rule that turns those marks into an alarm, such as a flame growing with no worker in the frame, is proposed as later work and was not built.
  • It was tested on pictures and recordings, not on a site.The scores come from a held-out part of the same collection. Use with a webcam or CCTV is the intended next step, and each site would need its own adjustment for light, camera angle and background.
  • Not every spark is a risk, and it cannot say which one is.Spatter loses energy in flight. Judging risk needs criteria for distance, speed and temperature at contact, which my own measurements could not supply.
  • People are still confused with background.The Engineer class kept the most confusion after the repairs. Welding, once boxed at the core, had the least.

No public source for 15 m was found in this check. The same KOSHA guideline posts a fire watch where combustibles in quantity lie within 11 m of the work.

A spark can smoulder inside combustible material for minutes to hours.0:33

Matched in part

The KOSHA guideline states that a spark can start a fire through stored heat a considerable time after it lands. It gives no time range, so “minutes to hours” rests on the film.

Burning urethane foam releases hydrogen cyanide.1:17

Matched to a source

A 2016 review in Fire Science Reviews names carbon monoxide and hydrogen cyanide as the two major asphyxiants from burning polyurethane foam, with yields that depend on ventilation. An earlier literature review of rigid foams by NBS, now NIST, reports the same pair.

McKenna & Hull (2016), Fire Science Reviews 5:3 ↗

Paabo & Levin (1985), NBSIR 85-3224 ↗

Matched to a source

National Fire Agency statistics for 2020 to 2024, as reported on 18 February 2025: 2,⁠732 fires, of which 2,⁠049 were attributed to carelessness and, within those, 1,⁠300 to welding, cutting and grinding. The film’s “sparks” is that last category.
